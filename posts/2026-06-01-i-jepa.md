- Do we really need hand-crafted augmentations to learn good image representations?
- Should it predict pixels, or something more abstract?
- What makes a prediction task teach semantics rather than texture?

[I-JEPA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.08243) (Assran, Duval, Misra, Bojanowski, Vincent,
Rabbat, LeCun and Ballas, January 2023) is the first working JEPA for images. It takes
the main idea of [A Path Towards Autonomous Machine Intelligence](../path-towards-autonomous-machine-intelligence/),
predicting in representation space rather than in pixel space, and turns it into
something you can train. Let's see how it learns from images without labels or hand-crafted
augmentations, and why predicting representations beats predicting pixels.

## Ways to learn from images

<figure>
  <img src="../../assets/posts/i-jepa/three-architectures.png" alt="Three self-supervised architectures side by side. (a) Joint-Embedding Architecture: x and y each go through their own encoder, and a distance D compares the two embeddings s-x and s-y. (b) Generative Architecture: x goes through an encoder and a decoder, conditioned on a latent z, to produce y-hat, which is compared to y itself. (c) Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture: x goes through an encoder and a predictor, conditioned on z, to produce a predicted embedding s-y-hat, which is compared to the embedding s-y from the y-encoder." width="3250" height="732" loading="lazy" decoding="async">
  <figcaption>Three ways to learn from images: compare embeddings, reconstruct the input, or predict embeddings. Figure from <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.08243">I-JEPA</a> (Assran et al., 2023).</figcaption>
</figure>

Before I-JEPA, self-supervised image models came in two families:

- **Invariance-based**: show the model two augmented views of one image (crops, colour
  jitter, blur) and make their embeddings agree (SimCLR, BYOL, DINO, iBOT). Strong
  semantic features, but the augmentations are hand-crafted, and they decide what the
  model is allowed to ignore.
- **Generative**: hide part of the image and reconstruct the missing pixels or tokens
  (MAE, BEiT). No heavy augmentations, but the model spends its capacity on low-level
  detail, and the features need fine-tuning to shine.

I-JEPA takes a third way and borrows one idea from each: it **masks** like the
generative methods, but **predicts representations** like the invariance methods.
Instead of reconstructing the missing part of the image, it predicts what that part
looks like in representation space. This is architecture (c) in the figure above.

## The architecture

Now let's look at how the authors turn this into a training signal, using only images and
no labels at all.

<figure>
  <img src="../../assets/posts/i-jepa/architecture.png" alt="The I-JEPA architecture. A context block of an image goes through the context encoder f-theta. The predictor g-phi takes its output together with mask tokens for three target blocks, shown in blue, red and yellow, and predicts a representation for each. The full image goes through the target encoder f-theta-bar, the outputs at the three target locations are selected, and each prediction is compared to its target with an L2 loss." width="1818" height="1760" loading="lazy" decoding="async">
  <figcaption>One context block predicts the representations of several target blocks. The targets come from encoding the whole image and selecting the target positions. Figure from <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.08243">I-JEPA</a> (Assran et al., 2023).</figcaption>
</figure>

Training runs in five steps:

{

The image is cut into patches. Four rectangular **target blocks** (each 15–20% of the
image) are the parts the model has to predict, and one large **context block** (85–100%
of the image) is the part it gets to see, minus any patches that overlap a target, so
it never sees the answer.

Note that the target blocks are taken from the *output* of the target encoder, not cut
out of the input image. The target encoder sees the whole image, so each target
representation already carries context from around it. This is what keeps the targets
at a high semantic level.

Random patches score less than a third of multi-block. Scattered
patches can be filled in from their neighbours, so the model never has to understand
the image.

}{cfyb card: Masking and target selection — 4 large target blocks, one big context block}

{

Do you really need to know what a ViT is? 😄

Fine: a ViT (Vision Transformer) is a stack of transformer layers, each a self-attention
block followed by a fully-connected MLP.

Here a ViT, $f_\theta$, encodes only the visible context patches. This is the encoder you
keep after training.

}{cfyb card: Context encoder — $f_\theta$, a ViT over the visible patches}

{

$f_{\bar\theta}$ encodes the *whole* image. It has the same architecture as the context
encoder, but it is not trained directly: its weights are an exponential moving average
(EMA) of the context encoder's, and it receives no gradients. The targets are its
outputs at the positions of each target block.

}{cfyb card: Target encoder — $f_{\bar\theta}$, EMA copy of $f_\theta$, sees the whole image}

{

$g_\phi$, a narrow ViT, takes the context encoder's output plus mask tokens that carry a
target block's position, and predicts the representations of that block. It runs once
for each of the four targets.

}{cfyb card: Predictor — $g_\phi$, narrow ViT, predicts each target}

{

You made it this far into a post about self-supervised learning and you need L2
explained? 🙃 No. Close this tab, go back to your first linear regression lecture, and
come back when you have made peace with it. 😡

}{cfyb card: Loss — L2 between predicted and target representations}

That is the whole method: no negative pairs, no pixel decoder, no colour jitter, no
random resized crop.

This design is what lets I-JEPA learn in representation space instead of pixel space:
the model never has to redraw an image, only predict what the hidden parts *mean*.

## Predicting representations, not pixels

The ablation that justifies the whole architecture compares two versions of the same
ViT-L/16: one where the predictor predicts the target encoder's representations, and one
where it predicts the raw pixels of the target blocks. The pixel version even trains
longer, 800 epochs against 500:

In pixel space the target is fixed by the data, so every unpredictable detail (texture,
lighting, noise) ends up in the loss. In representation space the target encoder
decides what is worth predicting, and that detail can simply be left out.

You can see this by decoding the predictor's output back into pixels with a separately
trained generative model. Each sample below is one guess at what fills the green box:

It beats MAE (77.2% with a ViT-H/14 after 1600 epochs) at a fraction of the compute, and
matches iBOT (81.0% with a ViT-L/16) only at the higher resolution. The claim is not
state of the art; it is competitive performance without hand-crafted views.

scale is exactly what depth depends on:

## Where it leads

I-JEPA showed that predicting in representation space works for images: no hand-crafted
augmentations, no pixel decoder, and strong features at a fraction of the compute.

One piece is still held together by engineering rather than theory. Nothing in the loss
stops both encoders from mapping every image to the same vector. What prevents that
collapse is the slow EMA target encoder, together with a few hyperparameters tuned
around it.

[LeJEPA](../lejepa/) later removed that piece. It drops the EMA teacher and adds
**SIGReg**, a regulariser that pushes the embeddings toward an isotropic Gaussian, so
collapse is ruled out by the objective itself rather than by the training dynamics.
