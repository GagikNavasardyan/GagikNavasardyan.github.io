- Which distribution *should* the latent space of a world model follow?
- How do you enforce that distribution on a multidimensional embedding?
- Do we need all the heuristics developed so far to avoid collapse?
- Is there a better and scalable alternative?

[LeJEPA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.08544) (Balestriero and LeCun, November 2025)
answers all four. The paper states four contributions:

1. The optimal embedding distribution for foundation models: the isotropic Gaussian.
2. **SIGReg**, a distribution-matching objective, provably correct and cheap at scale.
3. **LeJEPA**, a statistically optimal JEPA that cannot collapse by construction.
4. Validation across architectures and domains, and the case for in-domain pretraining.

Let's go through these contributions one by one, and uncover the intuition behind each.

## The heuristics

Joint-embedding methods learn by making two views of the same input agree in
representation space. The catch is that there is a trivial way to agree: map every input
to the same vector. Prediction is then perfect and the representation carries no
information. This is **collapse**, and it has shaped self-supervised learning for years.

To prevent it, the field built up a toolbox of fixes:

- **Negative pairs**: push apart embeddings of different inputs (SimCLR, MoCo).
- **EMA teacher**: targets from a slow-moving copy of the encoder (BYOL, DINO).
- **Stop-gradient**: block gradients through the target branch (BYOL, SimSiam).
- **Centering and sharpening**: normalise and temper the teacher's outputs (DINO).
- **Decorrelation**: spread and decorrelate the dimensions (VICReg, Barlow Twins).

Each of these works in practice, but each one blocks a symptom of collapse rather than
saying what a good embedding should look like. JEPAs inherited the same toolbox.
[I-JEPA](../i-jepa/) used an EMA target encoder and a stop-gradient;
[V-JEPA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.08471) kept both.

The problems show up at scale:

- **Many hyperparameters**: EMA momentum, temperatures and penalty weights, often each
  with its own schedule, and all of them interacting.
- **Limited transfer**: a change of architecture, domain or model size usually means
  finding the hyperparameter balance again.
- **Compute and memory**: contrastive methods need large batches of negatives, and a
  full covariance penalty grows quadratically with the embedding dimension.
- **Loss and quality are decoupled**: a collapsed model can reach a lower loss than a
  good one, so the health of a run is checked by probing with labels.

What is missing is a method that prevents collapse by argument rather than by trick,
one whose cost grows linearly and whose settings survive a change of scale. LeJEPA
starts from a different question: what distribution *should* the embeddings follow?
Once you have that answer, collapse stops being a failure mode you fight and becomes a
point in distribution space you are already far away from.

## Contribution 1: the optimal embedding distribution

Let's say we are training an encoder now, and someone will use its frozen embeddings for
a task later. We do not have any idea about the task, so here is the question to ask:
which embedding distribution minimises the **worst-case** downstream prediction risk?

The paper answers in two steps:

1. For linear probes, it proves that the embeddings need to be **isotropic**.
2. For some nonlinear probes, it proves that they also need to be **Gaussian**.

### Linear probes

A linear probe fits weights $\hat{\beta}$ on the frozen embeddings
$Z \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times K}$ to predict the labels $y$:

$$
\hat{\beta} = \arg\min_{\beta \in \mathbb{R}^K} \; \|y - Z\beta\|_2^2 + \alpha\|\beta\|_2^2
$$

With $\alpha = 0$ this is ordinary least squares (OLS); with $\alpha > 0$ it is ridge
regression. Now take two embeddings with the same total variance: $Z_{\text{iso}}$,
spread equally in every direction, and $Z_{\text{aniso}}$, whose covariance eigenvalues
$\lambda_1 \le \dots \le \lambda_K$ are unequal.

{

Whenever $\lambda_K > \lambda_1$, there always exists a downstream task for which
$Z_{\text{aniso}}$ yields a higher-bias estimator than $Z_{\text{iso}}$, for any ridge
penalty $\alpha > 0$.

}{cfyb card: Lemma 1 — anisotropy amplifies bias}

{

With $\alpha = 0$ (plain OLS), the total variance of $\hat{\beta}$ is minimised by the
isotropic embedding:
$\mathrm{tr}\big(\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\beta}_{\text{aniso}})\big) > \mathrm{tr}\big(\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\beta}_{\text{iso}})\big)$.

}{cfyb card: Lemma 2 — anisotropy amplifies variance}

The closed form of the OLS (and ridge) solution makes both proofs simple; they can be
found in the paper's appendix.

**In short:** Lemmas 1 and 2 give the theoretical motivation for isotropy. Any imbalance
between directions costs the linear probe in bias, in variance, or both, so the
embedding should spread its variance equally in every direction.

### Nonlinear probes

For $k$-NN and kernel-based probes, the paper proves that isotropy is not enough: among
all distributions with the same total variance, the isotropic Gaussian is the unique
minimiser.

{

For $k$-NN and kernel-based probes, among distributions subject to a fixed
total-variance constraint, the isotropic Gaussian is the **unique** minimiser of the
integrated squared bias.

}{cfyb card: Theorem 1 — isotropic Gaussian optimality}

The proof is in the paper's appendix. Note that it covers these two nonlinear cases
only.

{

**Gaussianity rests on a narrow base.** The linear-probe lemmas only give us isotropy.
The step to *Gaussian* comes from Theorem 1, which covers two probe families, $k$-NN and
kernel-based. To be honest, these are not what people usually put on top of a
foundation model in practice. On its own, that is not enough to conclude that
embeddings should be Gaussian.

**A second argument, from world models.**
[When Does LeJEPA Learn a World Model?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26379) (Klindt, LeCun
and Balestriero, 2026) reaches the Gaussian from a different direction. It shows that a JEPA whose embeddings
are kept isotropic Gaussian recovers the true latent variables of the world up to a
rotation, and that the Gaussian is the *only* latent distribution for which this kind of
linear recovery is possible.

These theorems hold under assumptions: latent dimensions that evolve independently,
the same distribution for both views, and transitions with additive noise. Real data
rarely satisfies all three, so some questions stay open.

**The intuition I keep coming back to: maximum entropy.** Among all distributions with
the same total variance, the isotropic Gaussian has the highest entropy. It commits to
the least structure the budget allows. If we have no idea what the downstream task will
be, that is exactly what we want: keep the embeddings as general as possible. A good
embedding then does two things at once. It keeps similar examples close together, and it
spreads everything out to push the overall entropy up, so the embedding space holds as
much generic information as possible for whatever task comes later.

**What if we do know something about the task?** In practice we often know the domain,
or roughly which tasks the model will serve. Optimising for the worst case over *all*
tasks then throws that knowledge away. How would the optimal distribution change?
Would it become anisotropic along the directions that matter, or not Gaussian at all? I would be
curious to see work on this.

### What this means for collapse

Collapse stops being a separate phenomenon requiring a separate mechanism. A collapsed
encoder produces a degenerate distribution, which is simply the most extreme way of
missing the isotropic Gaussian. Anisotropy is bad for exactly the
same reason, just less dramatically. One quantity explains both the catastrophic failure
and the everyday mediocrity.

## Contribution 2: SIGReg

Knowing the target distribution is one thing. Enforcing it on a high-dimensional
embedding at every training step is another: estimating a high-dimensional density
directly is far too expensive.

**Sketched Isotropic Gaussian Regularization** (SIGReg) avoids that with one idea: a
distribution is fully determined by its one-dimensional projections (the Cramér–Wold
theorem). So instead of testing in $K$ dimensions, test in one dimension, many times:

1. Sample random directions.
2. Project the batch of embeddings onto each direction.
3. Measure how far each projection is from a standard normal, and average.

<figure>
  <img src="../../assets/posts/lejepa/sigreg.png" alt="Left: a two-dimensional embedding distribution shown as a heat map of scattered modes, with three coloured arrows marking random projection directions. Right: the three corresponding one-dimensional projected densities, each plotted against the standard normal, with the shaded area between them marked as error." width="1052" height="558" loading="lazy" decoding="async">
  <figcaption>SIGReg in one picture. Each random direction turns the embedding cloud into a one-dimensional density; the shaded area between that density and the standard normal is the penalty. Enough directions and nothing but an isotropic Gaussian survives. Figure from <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.08544">LeJEPA</a> (Balestriero and LeCun, 2025).</figcaption>
</figure>

One direction constrains almost nothing. With enough random directions, the only
distribution that passes all of them is the isotropic Gaussian.

For step 3 the paper uses the **Epps–Pulley** test, which compares characteristic
functions. It is chosen for training rather than for statistics: its gradients are
bounded, so the regulariser cannot blow up, and it needs no sorting. The whole thing
runs in linear time and memory and splits easily across GPUs.

{

To me, this is a genuinely beautiful algorithm. The idea fits in a sentence, every step
has a clear reason behind it, and the result is both provably correct and cheap enough
to run at scale. It is honestly one of the reasons I got interested in JEPA-style
pretraining, and in self-supervised learning more generally.

The heuristics that came before always felt a little unsatisfying to me. Each one
worked, but they read like patches added one at a time, and it was hard to say why that
particular combination was the right one. SIGReg shows that it can be done differently:
decide what the embeddings should look like, enforce exactly that, and collapse is
handled as a consequence rather than as a separate fight.

## Contribution 3: LeJEPA

Now combine the two, and the architecture is almost embarrassingly plain:

1. Take an image and make several augmented views of it: a couple of large *global*
   views and several small *local* ones.
2. Encode every view with **the same encoder**.
3. **Prediction:** pull every view's embedding toward the average embedding of the
   global views.
4. **SIGReg:** push the embeddings toward an isotropic Gaussian.

The loss is just the sum of the two, with a single weight:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{LeJEPA}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}} + \lambda \cdot \mathrm{SIGReg}
$$

That weight is the only hyperparameter, and $\lambda = 0.05$ works across the board. The
whole implementation is about 50 lines of PyTorch.

Collapse is now ruled out by the objective itself. Prediction alone would be happiest
with a constant encoder, since then every view matches perfectly. But a constant
embedding looks nothing like a Gaussian along any direction, so SIGReg does not allow
it.

Here is what that deletes:

| Removed | Why it is no longer needed |
| --- | --- |
| Stop-gradient | Bounded gradients; nothing to protect against |
| Teacher–student / EMA | No asymmetry required: one encoder, all views |
| Predictor network | Views predict the global mean directly |
| Variance + covariance terms | Subsumed by matching the full distribution |
| Temperature and momentum schedules | Nothing to anneal |
| Per-architecture tuning | Same $\lambda$ across families (see below) |
