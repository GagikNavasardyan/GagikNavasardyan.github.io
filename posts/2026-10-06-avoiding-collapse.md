This post keeps track of the methods proposed to avoid **collapse** in self-supervised
learning. It is meant for a quick look at their history, with a short description of
each one.

## The collapse problem

Many self-supervised methods train a network to give similar embeddings to two related
inputs, such as two crops of the same image. The easiest way to do that is to ignore the
input and always output the same vector. The loss is minimised, but the embeddings carry
no information. This is collapse, and every method below adds something to prevent it.

{

Collapse does not have to be total. In **dimensional collapse**, the embeddings do not
shrink to a single point, but they only spread along a few directions: a 2048-dimensional
embedding may effectively use a few dozen. Training looks healthy, the loss is fine, and
the representation is still much poorer than it could be.

It is subtler and more common than complete collapse. Even methods designed to avoid
collapse can suffer from it. It is one reason why methods that constrain the *whole*
distribution of embeddings, not just a constant output, came to matter.

## A short history

Each milestone below opens with a short explanation. Filled dots mark the turning points.

<ol class="timeline" aria-label="Timeline of methods for avoiding collapse">
  <li class="timeline-item"><span class="timeline-year">1993</span><span class="timeline-dot" aria-hidden="true"></span><details class="timeline-details"><summary><span class="timeline-title">Siamese networks</span><span class="bar-tag">Contrastive</span><span class="timeline-text">Two copies of one network compare two inputs.</span></summary><div class="timeline-body"><p>Bromley and colleagues verified signatures with two copies of the same network, sharing their weights. Matching signatures should land close together, forgeries far apart.</p><p>From the very start, the network was shown <em>negative</em> pairs (forgeries), so it could not simply map everything to one point.</p></div></details></li>
  <li class="timeline-item"><span class="timeline-year">2006</span><span class="timeline-dot" aria-hidden="true"></span><details class="timeline-details"><summary><span class="timeline-title">Contrastive loss (DrLIM)</span><span class="bar-tag">Contrastive</span><span class="timeline-text">Pull matching pairs together, push the rest past a margin.</span></summary><div class="timeline-body"><p>Hadsell, Chopra and LeCun turned the idea into a general loss: similar pairs are pulled together, dissimilar pairs are pushed apart until they are at least a margin away.</p><p>It is the template for everything contrastive that followed: collapse is avoided because negatives must stay apart.</p></div></details></li>
  <li class="timeline-item"><span class="timeline-year">2018</span><span class="timeline-dot" aria-hidden="true"></span><details class="timeline-details"><summary><span class="timeline-title">DeepCluster</span><span class="bar-tag">Clustering</span><span class="timeline-text">Learn by predicting cluster labels of your own features.</span></summary><div class="timeline-body"><p>Caron and colleagues alternate between clustering the network's features with k-means and training the network to predict those cluster labels.</p><p>Here collapse means everything falling into one cluster. The fix is practical: empty clusters are re-seeded and training samples are balanced across clusters.</p></div></details></li>
  <li class="timeline-item"><span class="timeline-year">2018</span><span class="timeline-dot" aria-hidden="true"></span><details class="timeline-details"><summary><span class="timeline-title">Memory banks and InfoNCE</span><span class="bar-tag">Contrastive</span><span class="timeline-text">Score the right match against many negatives at once.</span></summary><div class="timeline-body"><p>Instance discrimination (Wu and colleagues) treats every image as its own class and keeps a <strong>memory bank</strong> of stored embeddings to use as negatives.</p><p>CPC (van den Oord and colleagues) introduced the <strong>InfoNCE</strong> loss: a softmax that scores the true match against many negatives. It became the standard contrastive loss.</p></div></details></li>
  <li class="timeline-item"><span class="timeline-year">2019</span><span class="timeline-dot" aria-hidden="true"></span><details class="timeline-details"><summary><span class="timeline-title">MoCo</span><span class="bar-tag">Contrastive</span><span class="timeline-text">A queue of negatives, encoded by a slow momentum encoder.</span></summary><div class="timeline-body"><p>He and colleagues keep a <strong>queue</strong> of negatives from past batches. To keep old and new negatives comparable, they are encoded by a <strong>momentum encoder</strong>, a slowly updated (EMA) copy of the network.</p><p>This is where the EMA copy first appears in contrastive learning (Mean Teacher had used one in 2017 for semi-supervised learning); it later becomes the main anti-collapse tool of its own.</p></div></details></li>
  <li class="timeline-item is-key"><span class="timeline-year">2020</span><span class="timeline-dot" aria-hidden="true"></span><details class="timeline-details"><summary><span class="timeline-title">SimCLR</span><span class="bar-tag">Contrastive</span><span class="timeline-text">Negatives from a huge batch, plus strong augmentations.</span></summary><div class="timeline-body"><p>Chen and colleagues drop the queue: every other image in a very large batch is a negative. Strong augmentations and a small projection head on top of the encoder make it work.</p><p>The cost of the contrastive family shows clearly here: good results need thousands of negatives per step, and two photos of different dogs are pushed apart as if they had nothing in common.</p></div></details></li>
  <li class="timeline-item is-key"><span class="timeline-year">2020</span><span class="timeline-dot" aria-hidden="true"></span><details class="timeline-details"><summary><span class="timeline-title">BYOL</span><span class="bar-tag">Asymmetry</span><span class="timeline-text">No negatives at all: an EMA teacher, a predictor and a stop-gradient.</span></summary><div class="timeline-body"><p>Grill and colleagues showed negatives are not needed. An <strong>online</strong> network with an extra predictor head learns to match a <strong>target</strong> network, which is an EMA copy of the online one and receives no gradients (a <strong>stop-gradient</strong>).</p><p>The loss still allows collapse, but in practice training does not fall into it. Why exactly was debated for years.</p></div></details></li>
  <li class="timeline-item"><span class="timeline-year">2020</span><span class="timeline-dot" aria-hidden="true"></span><details class="timeline-details"><summary><span class="timeline-title">SwAV</span><span class="bar-tag">Clustering</span><span class="timeline-text">Predict cluster codes across views, with clusters forced to be used evenly.</span></summary><div class="timeline-body"><p>Caron and colleagues cluster online: the cluster code of one view is predicted from another view. The Sinkhorn–Knopp algorithm forces each batch to spread <strong>evenly</strong> across all clusters, so everything cannot fall into one.</p><p>The cost of the clustering family: the number of clusters and the balancing settings become new hyperparameters.</p></div></details></li>
  <li class="timeline-item"><span class="timeline-year">2020</span><span class="timeline-dot" aria-hidden="true"></span><details class="timeline-details"><summary><span class="timeline-title">SimSiam</span><span class="bar-tag">Asymmetry</span><span class="timeline-text">A stop-gradient and a predictor are enough, no EMA needed.</span></summary><div class="timeline-body"><p>Chen and He removed the EMA teacher: two identical branches, a predictor on one side and a stop-gradient on the other.</p><p>Remove the stop-gradient and it collapses at once, so the stop-gradient is what holds it up, not the EMA.</p></div></details></li>
  <li class="timeline-item"><span class="timeline-year">2020</span><span class="timeline-dot" aria-hidden="true"></span><details class="timeline-details"><summary><span class="timeline-title">W-MSE</span><span class="bar-tag">Regularisation</span><span class="timeline-text">Whiten every batch before comparing views.</span></summary><div class="timeline-body"><p>Ermolov and colleagues <strong>whiten</strong> the embeddings of each batch (zero mean, identity covariance) before comparing the two views. A whitened batch cannot collapse, by construction.</p><p>It opens the regularisation family: instead of relying on dynamics, constrain the embeddings directly.</p></div></details></li>
  <li class="timeline-item"><span class="timeline-year">2021</span><span class="timeline-dot" aria-hidden="true"></span><details class="timeline-details"><summary><span class="timeline-title">Barlow Twins</span><span class="bar-tag">Regularisation</span><span class="timeline-text">Push the cross-correlation between views toward the identity.</span></summary><div class="timeline-body"><p>Zbontar and colleagues compute the cross-correlation matrix between the two views' embeddings and push it toward the <strong>identity</strong>.</p><p>The diagonal makes each dimension agree with itself across views; the off-diagonal makes different dimensions carry different information, which also fights dimensional collapse.</p></div></details></li>
  <li class="timeline-item"><span class="timeline-year">2021</span><span class="timeline-dot" aria-hidden="true"></span><details class="timeline-details"><summary><span class="timeline-title">DINO</span><span class="bar-tag">Asymmetry</span><span class="timeline-text">An EMA teacher kept balanced by centering and sharpening.</span></summary><div class="timeline-body"><p>Caron and colleagues train a student to match the output distribution of an EMA teacher. Two opposite tricks keep it stable: <strong>centering</strong> subtracts a running mean so no single output dominates, and <strong>sharpening</strong> uses a low temperature so the outputs do not become uniform.</p><p>Each trick alone leads to collapse; balanced against each other, they hold.</p></div></details></li>
  <li class="timeline-item is-key"><span class="timeline-year">2021</span><span class="timeline-dot" aria-hidden="true"></span><details class="timeline-details"><summary><span class="timeline-title">VICReg</span><span class="bar-tag">Regularisation</span><span class="timeline-text">Three explicit terms: variance, invariance and covariance.</span></summary><div class="timeline-body"><p>Bardes, Ponce and LeCun split the job into three terms: keep every dimension's spread above a threshold (<strong>variance</strong>), make the two views close (<strong>invariance</strong>), and decorrelate the dimensions (<strong>covariance</strong>).</p><p>It is the method the <a href="../path-towards-autonomous-machine-intelligence/">Path Towards Autonomous Machine Intelligence</a> paper recommends for training a JEPA. Its limit: it controls only the mean and covariance, and the covariance term grows quadratically with the embedding size.</p></div></details></li>
  <li class="timeline-item"><span class="timeline-year">2021</span><span class="timeline-dot" aria-hidden="true"></span><details class="timeline-details"><summary><span class="timeline-title">MAE</span><span class="bar-tag">Reconstruction</span><span class="timeline-text">Reconstruct masked pixels. A fixed target cannot collapse.</span></summary><div class="timeline-body"><p>He and colleagues hide 75% of an image's patches and reconstruct the missing pixels. The target is the image itself, so there is nothing to collapse: no negatives, no EMA.</p><p>The price is semantics: the model spends its capacity on texture and lighting. <a href="../i-jepa/">I-JEPA</a> shows that predicting representations instead of pixels is worth 26 points in the same setup.</p></div></details></li>
  <li class="timeline-item"><span class="timeline-year">2023</span><span class="timeline-dot" aria-hidden="true"></span><details class="timeline-details"><summary><span class="timeline-title">I-JEPA and DINOv2</span><span class="bar-tag">Asymmetry</span><span class="timeline-text">EMA teachers at scale; DINOv2 adds a term that spreads embeddings out.</span></summary><div class="timeline-body"><p><a href="../i-jepa/">I-JEPA</a> and later V-JEPA predict in representation space with exactly the BYOL-style machinery: an EMA target encoder and a stop-gradient.</p><p>DINOv2 scales up DINO and adds the <strong>KoLeo</strong> regulariser, which pushes the embeddings in a batch apart so they cover the space evenly. Effective, but the stack of tricks keeps growing.</p></div></details></li>
  <li class="timeline-item is-key"><span class="timeline-year">2025</span><span class="timeline-dot" aria-hidden="true"></span><details class="timeline-details"><summary><span class="timeline-title">LeJEPA and SIGReg</span><span class="bar-tag">Distribution matching</span><span class="timeline-text">Make embeddings an isotropic Gaussian, and collapse is ruled out by design.</span></summary><div class="timeline-body"><p><a href="../lejepa/">LeJEPA</a> asks what distribution the embeddings <em>should</em> follow, and proves it is the <strong>isotropic Gaussian</strong>. <strong>SIGReg</strong> enforces it by testing many random 1-D projections against a standard normal, which covers all moments, not just the first two.</p><p>A constant embedding, or one squeezed onto a few directions, cannot look Gaussian, so collapse is excluded by the objective itself. No negatives, no EMA, no stop-gradient, one hyperparameter.</p></div></details></li>
  <li class="timeline-item"><span class="timeline-year">2026</span><span class="timeline-dot" aria-hidden="true"></span><details class="timeline-details"><summary><span class="timeline-title">LeWorldModel</span><span class="bar-tag">Distribution matching</span><span class="timeline-text">SIGReg carried into world models.</span></summary><div class="timeline-body"><p><a href="../leworldmodel/">LeWorldModel</a> uses SIGReg to train a world model end-to-end from pixels, something that previously needed a frozen encoder or six extra loss terms.</p></div></details></li>
</ol>

## Six ways to stop it

Seen together, the fixes fall into six families. Each blocks collapse in a different way,
and each brings its own cost:

| Family | How collapse is prevented | Main cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contrastive | Negatives are pushed apart | Many negatives, false negatives |
| Clustering | Clusters must be used evenly | Extra hyperparameters |
| Asymmetry | EMA teacher, predictor, stop-gradient | Works by dynamics, not by argument |
| Regularisation | Variance and covariance constraints | Only the first two moments |
| Reconstruction | Target fixed by the pixels | Low-level, less semantic features |
| Distribution matching | Embeddings must be isotropic Gaussian | One weight to set |
