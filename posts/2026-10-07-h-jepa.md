How would you plan a trip to the top of a mountain if you could only think one step ahead?
You would probably struggle, and end up with the strategy of going up at every step: take
the stairs or the elevator to the top of your building, then sit on the roof and wait for evolution to
give you wings. What we actually do is plan in days, then in hours, then in steps. We
set realistic milestones and destinations at several levels of abstraction, all the way
from our home to the top of the mountain.

A world model that plans far ahead needs
the same thing, reasoning at several timescales at once. [A Path Towards Autonomous Machine Intelligence](../path-towards-autonomous-machine-intelligence/)
proposed a way to do this with a hierarchy of JEPAs: lower levels keep detail and
predict a short time ahead, higher levels keep only what stays predictable and look
further ahead, and planning flows from the top down. If we sit back and think about it, the brain is organised in a similar
way, with slower-changing representations higher up.

[H-JEPA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06805) (Zhang, Terver, Rabbat, LeCun and Balestriero,
October 2026) turns this hierarchy idea into a working model: it trains several distinct levels of abstraction end to end,
on top of [LeWorldModel](../leworldmodel/), and plans through them from the top down.

## Three types of abstraction

To see what H-JEPA adds, the paper puts it next to two other world models. Here is how
each of the three would climb our mountain:

- **[LeWorldModel](../leworldmodel/) (LeWM): one level.** It plans the climb a few
  footsteps at a time. Every plan is a long chain of small steps, so
  mistakes pile up along the way and the number of possible routes explodes. Its single
  latent space also has to do two jobs: describe each foot placement, and tell how far
  you still are from the summit.
- **[HWM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.03208): several levels, one
  shared latent space.** It also plans in big jumps. A high-level predictor imagines
  where a whole day of walking will take you, and those points become camps: subgoals
  for a low-level predictor that plans the steps to the next camp. But every camp is
  described in the same detail as a footstep, down to where your feet stand.
- **H-JEPA: several levels, each with its own latent space.** The first level is a LeWM
  and plans the footsteps exactly as before. Above it, each level has its own encoder and
  learns its own, more abstract picture of the climb. At the top, the climb is a short
  list of landmarks: the hut at the treeline, the ridge, the summit. Each level passes its
  next landmark down as a subgoal, and the first level works out the footsteps to reach
  it.

So LeWM → HWM shows what splitting the horizon gives, and HWM → H-JEPA shows what separate
latent spaces add.

## How it works

Every level of H-JEPA is a small world model of its own, built from the same three
modules. The figure shows two levels, with $\ell$ the level number:

<figure>
  <img src="../../assets/posts/h-jepa/architecture.png" alt="Left, building the hierarchy: at level 1, state encoders E1 turn observations o0 to o3 into latent states z0 to z3, and action encoders A1 turn actions a0 and a1 into action embeddings. At level 2, a state encoder E2 reads only every second level-1 state, z0 and z2, and an action encoder A2 merges the two level-1 action embeddings into one. Right, training each level: a predictor F takes the latent state z_t and action a_t and predicts the next latent state, compared with the true next state; SIGReg regularises the latent states, and the per-level loss is the prediction loss plus lambda times SIGReg." width="1718" height="684" loading="lazy" decoding="async">
  <figcaption>H-JEPA training across temporal scales. Figure from <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06805">H-JEPA</a> (Zhang et al., 2026).</figcaption>
</figure>

- **State encoder $E^{(\ell)}$** turns what the level sees into a latent state
  $z^{(\ell)}$. At level 1, it is LeWorldModel's ViT and reads a camera frame $o_t$. At
  each level above, it is a small two-layer MLP that reads a latent state of the level
  below, but only every second one. In the figure, level 2 reads $z_0^{(1)}$ and
  $z_2^{(1)}$ and skips the faded $z_1^{(1)}$ and $z_3^{(1)}$, so it moves through time in
  steps twice as long as level 1.
- **Action encoder $A^{(\ell)}$** turns actions into action embeddings $a^{(\ell)}$. At
  level 1, it embeds the robot's actual action. At each level above, it merges the two
  action embeddings of the level below that happen between two of its states into a
  single macro-action, an idea from HWM. In the figure, $a_0^{(1)}$ and $a_1^{(1)}$
  become $a_0^{(2)}$.
- **Predictor $F^{(\ell)}$** is the world model of the level. From the latent states so
  far and their actions, it predicts the next latent state $\hat z_{t+1}^{(\ell)}$.

Level 1 on its own is exactly a [LeWorldModel](../leworldmodel/). Level 2 is built on
level 1's latent states, level 3 on level 2's, and so on, so each level's predictor looks
twice as far ahead as the one below it.

Each level is trained with the same two-term loss as LeWorldModel:

$$
\mathcal{L}^{(\ell)} = \mathcal{L}^{(\ell)}_{\text{pred}} + \lambda_\ell \cdot \mathrm{SIGReg}\big(Z^{(\ell)}\big)
$$

Here $\ell$ is the level, $Z^{(\ell)}$ its latents and $\lambda_\ell$ the weight of its
regulariser. The first term asks the level's predictor to land on its next latent; the
second, [SIGReg](../lejepa/), keeps the level's latents close to an isotropic Gaussian, so
none of them can collapse. The total loss is simply the sum of these per-level losses.
All levels are trained together, end to end: the losses of the
upper levels also shape the encoders below them. For visually diverse data, such as real
robot videos, an inverse-dynamics term is added, which predicts the action taken
between two consecutive latents.

Planning runs from the top down. The current observation and the goal image are encoded
at every level $\ell$, giving the current latent $z_0^{(\ell)}$ and the goal latent
$g^{(\ell)}$. The top level $L$ searches for macro-actions whose final predicted latent
$\hat z^{(L)}$ gets closest to $g^{(L)}$, and its first predicted latent $\hat z_1^{(L)}$
becomes the subgoal of the level below.

That subgoal lives in the upper level's latent space, while the level below predicts
latents $\hat z^{(\ell)}$ in its own, so the two cannot be compared directly. To measure
its progress, the lower level passes its own predictions through the upper level's state
encoder $E^{(\ell+1)}$, and computes the distance to the subgoal in the upper space:

$$
\Big\| \, E^{(\ell+1)}\big(\hat z^{(\ell)}\big) - \hat z_1^{(\ell+1)} \Big\|_2^2
$$

Here $\hat z^{(\ell)}$ is the lower level's predicted latent at the moment it should reach
the subgoal, $E^{(\ell+1)}$ lifts it into the upper space, and $\hat z_1^{(\ell+1)}$ is the
subgoal handed down from the level above. This is where training all levels together pays
off: the upper encoders that the lower planners rely on were learned with them. Each level
repeats this with the subgoal it receives, down to the first level, which returns real
actions.

At every level, the actions are optimised by gradient descent: the distance to the goal
or subgoal is backpropagated through the frozen predictor to the actions, while the
model's weights stay fixed. The agent executes the first few actions, observes the result
and plans again.

## What each level keeps, and what it drops

To see what a level has learned, the authors freeze the trained model and fit small probes
that try to read physical quantities out of each level's latents, such as the ant's
position or the angles of its legs. A low probe error means the quantity is still in the
latent; an error near 1 means it is gone.

<figure>
  <img src="../../assets/posts/h-jepa/depth-probes.png" alt="Left: probe error against hierarchy level. In Visual AntMaze, the ant's body configuration becomes harder to read out at higher levels while its position stays perfectly readable; in FourRoom, the distractor becomes harder to read out while the agent's own position stays readable. Right: one observation decoded from levels 1 to 3, becoming blurrier in fast-changing details." width="864" height="300" loading="lazy" decoding="async">
  <figcaption>Selective abstraction across hierarchy levels. Figure from <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06805">H-JEPA</a> (Zhang et al., 2026).</figcaption>
</figure>

The upper levels keep some things and drop others. In Visual AntMaze, the ant's position
can be read out at every level, but the pose of its legs gets harder to recover the higher
you go. It is the same on our mountain: a plan for the day cares about where you will
camp tonight, not about where you put each foot. In FourRoom, the agent's own position
stays, while the distractor that moves around on its own fades away, just as the day
plan does not care about the wooden stick lying on the path that you will have to step
over. That is left to the footsteps. The authors also decoded each level's latents
back into images, only for visualization: at higher levels the ant's legs become less
distinct and the distractor fades, while the agent stays visible.

The upper levels also give the planner a better sense of direction. To plan, a level
measures how far it is from the goal as the Euclidean (L2) distance between latents. The figure below
maps this distance over the whole of Visual AntMaze, for the three levels of one trained
model. Every dot is a position of the ant, coloured by how far its latent is from the
latent of the starred position.

<figure>
  <img src="../../assets/posts/h-jepa/composite-levels.png" alt="Three heatmaps of a maze, coloured by latent distance to a starred anchor point. At level 1 almost everything is equally far. At levels 2 and 3 the distance grows smoothly outward from the anchor." width="975" height="479" loading="lazy" decoding="async">
  <figcaption>Planning cost geometry on AntMaze. Figure from <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06805">H-JEPA</a> (Zhang et al., 2026).</figcaption>
</figure>

At level 1, almost the whole maze is the same red: apart from the area right around the
star, every position looks equally far away, so a planner gets no hint of which way to go
until it is already close. At levels 2 and 3, the colour changes gradually along the
corridors, from blue near the star through yellow to red at the far ends. Moving towards
the star through the maze steadily lowers the distance, which is a cost a planner can
follow from far away. This is how the upper levels can lead the levels below towards a
distant goal.

## Results

All three models are tested in four simulated environments: two navigation tasks,
FourRoom, where a distractor moves around independently of the agent, and Visual
AntMaze, where a four-legged ant crosses a maze; and two manipulation tasks, Push-T and
OGBench Cube. In every task the model gets the current image and a goal image, and has to
plan its way there within a fixed budget of steps.

The first comparison asks how success grows with planning effort. The paper varies how
many action sequences the planner optimises, and plots success against the compute this
costs, in TFLOPs per episode:

In FourRoom, AntMaze and Cube, each added level moves the curve up and to the left: more
success for less compute. On Visual AntMaze, the longest-horizon task, three-level H-JEPA
reaches 73% at about 100 TFLOPs per episode, while flat LeWM stays near 30% even with ten
times more compute, about 1,000 TFLOPs. Push-T is the exception: two levels match or beat LeWM at every budget, but three levels
do poorly. One possible explanation, given by the authors, is that Push-T episodes are too
short to train a three-level model well.

The second comparison brings in HWM and varies the number of levels, on a separate set of
50 tasks per environment, with the planner tuned under a budget of 100 TFLOPs. Here HWM
uses exactly the same code, data and training as H-JEPA, except that every level above the
first keeps level 1's latent space:

<figure>
  <img src="../../assets/posts/h-jepa/depth-lewm-hwm-hjepa.png" alt="Four line plots of success rate against number of levels, one per environment, for LeWM (flat, one level), HWM and H-JEPA. FourRoom: both hierarchies rise from about 41 percent to about 96 to 99 percent at three levels. Visual AntMaze: H-JEPA rises to 73 percent at three levels while HWM stays below 40. OGBench Cube: H-JEPA reaches about 60 to 63 percent, HWM peaks at about 53. Push-T: both hierarchies fall to near zero at four levels." width="1668" height="491" loading="lazy" decoding="async">
  <figcaption>Success by number of levels for LeWM, HWM and H-JEPA. Figure from <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06805">H-JEPA</a> (Zhang et al., 2026).</figcaption>
</figure>

- Both hierarchies beat flat LeWM in FourRoom, AntMaze and Cube, so splitting the
  horizon helps on its own. With three levels, H-JEPA takes Visual AntMaze from 18.0% to
  73.3%, FourRoom from 40.7% to 96.0% and OGBench Cube from 32.0% to 60.0%. On Push-T, two
  levels do a little better than LeWM (45.3% against 40.0%), and more levels fall below it.
- Separate latent spaces add most on Visual AntMaze, where three-level H-JEPA reaches
  nearly twice HWM's success (73% against about 38%). They add a little on OGBench Cube,
  and on FourRoom and Push-T the two hierarchies are about the same.

  <figcaption>Scene diversity makes the inverse-dynamics loss necessary, and a second level adds performance at a lower planner budget. Figure from <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06805">H-JEPA</a> (Zhang et al., 2026).</figcaption>

This variety creates a trap. Within one video the scene does not change, so it is easy to
predict, while the arm only moves in response to the actions. A model trained only to
predict its next latent can take a shortcut: remember which scene it is in and ignore the
arm. Its predictions are then almost perfect, but it learns nothing about how to act; the
paper calls this slow-feature collapse. The fix is an extra inverse-dynamics loss: from two
consecutive latents, a small head has to guess the action taken between them, which it can
only do if the latents keep track of the arm. At each level, it is added to the loss above:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{idm}} = \frac{1}{T-1} \sum_{t=1}^{T-1} \frac{1}{d_a} \Big\| \, I\big(z_t, z_{t+1}\big) - a_t \Big\|_2^2
$$

Here $z_t$ and $z_{t+1}$ are two consecutive latents of a clip of length $T$, $I$ is the
inverse-dynamics head that guesses the action between them, $a_t$ is the action actually
taken, used as a fixed target (a macro-action at the upper levels), and $d_a$ is the number of action dimensions.

DROID has no simulator, so plans cannot be executed. Instead, each planned arm path is
compared with the path the expert actually took. The comparison uses the Fréchet
distance $d_F$ between two paths: walk along both from start to end, each at your own
pace, and $d_F$ is the shortest leash that keeps you connected the whole way. The score
measures how much of the gap to the expert a plan closes, compared with not moving at
all:

$$
\text{fidelity} = \frac{d_F(0, \tilde p) - d_F(p, \tilde p)}{d_F(0, \tilde p)}
$$

Here $p$ is the planned path of the robot's hand in 3D, $\tilde p$ the expert's path, and
$0$ the path of not moving at all. A fidelity of 0% is as good as standing still, 100%
matches the expert, and a negative value is worse than doing nothing.

Panel (b) shows the effect. Without the inverse-dynamics loss, flat LeWM collapses to
about 0%. With it, it reaches 34%. A second level adds to that: HWM reaches 35% and
H-JEPA 40%, so separate latent spaces help on real scenes too. Panel (c) adds the planning
compute: H-JEPA gets its fidelity with less compute than the flat model, while two world
models built on frozen pretrained encoders, JEPA-WM and V-JEPA 2-AC, need orders of
magnitude more compute for lower fidelity. No physical robot was used; all of these
results are offline.

## Future directions

- Closed-loop control on a physical robot: the DROID results are offline; next is running
  the plans on a real robot.
- Learning from video without actions: building hierarchies from action-free video, which
  is much easier to collect.
- Goals given in words: aligning upper levels with language, so a goal can be described
  instead of shown as an image.
- Segments of varying length: letting each level learn how far ahead to look, instead of
  always twice the level below.
