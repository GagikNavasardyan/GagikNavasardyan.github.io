- Is a latent space that avoids collapse also a good space to plan in?
- If the predictor is perfect, can a world model still choose the wrong plan?
- Do the latents of a world model really have to be an isotropic Gaussian?

[Anisotropic Representations Improve Planning in JEPA World Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.37441)
(Kang, Oh, Kim and Lee, September 2026) studies these questions for world models like
[LeWorldModel](../leworldmodel/), which plan by Euclidean distance in a latent space
shaped by SIGReg. The paper makes three contributions:

1. A proof, in a linear setting, that accurate prediction and no collapse are not enough:
   with SIGReg, latent distance can rank plans differently from the task.
2. ΛReg, which replaces SIGReg's isotropic target with a Gaussian whose variance in each
   direction is learned, while prediction and planning stay the same.
3. AnisoWM, LeWorldModel trained with ΛReg, which plans more successfully than
   LeWorldModel on all four benchmarks tested.

In the [LeJEPA post](../lejepa/) we asked how the optimal embedding distribution would
change if we knew something about the downstream task. Here the task is known (planning
by latent distance), and the paper places a simple bet: keep the Gaussian, but let every
direction pick its own variance.

## Why an isotropic latent can mislead a planner

LeWorldModel scores a sequence of actions $U$ by how close its predicted final latent lands
to the goal:

$$
J_z(U) = \big\| \hat z_H(U) - z_g \big\|^2
$$

Here $H$ is the planning horizon, $\hat z_H(U)$ the latent the predictor reaches after
applying the actions $U$, and $z_g$ the latent of the goal image. The planner only cares
about the order this cost puts plans in. So the question is whether that order matches the
order of the real task cost, for example the physical distance to the goal.

### A robot vacuum with a bent ruler

Take a robot vacuum in a room twice as long as it is wide, drawn below with the long side
left to right. In the training videos it wanders everywhere, so its position
$x = (x_1, x_2)$ spreads twice as much left–right as up–down: a standard deviation of 2 m
left–right and 1 m up–down. Its wheels also slip a little, by the same amount in every
direction.

SIGReg asks every latent direction to have the same spread. The simplest encoder that does
this divides each coordinate by its spread:

$$
z = \Big( \frac{x_1}{2},\ \frac{x_2}{1} \Big)
$$

Here $x_1$ is the left–right position and $x_2$ the up–down position, in metres. Both
latent coordinates now have spread 1, as SIGReg asks. But the ruler of the latent space has
changed: a metre left–right is half a unit, while a metre up–down is a full unit.

Now put the goal far away and give the planner too few steps to get there. It has to stop
somewhere short, and it compares two places where it can stop: A, 1.5 m to the left of the
goal, and B, 1 m below it.

A's 1.5 m left–right shrinks to $1.5 / 2 = 0.75$ units, while B's 1 m up–down stays 1 unit,
so A looks closer. ($J_z$ squares the distance, which does not change which one is

|---|---|---|---|
| Real, in metres | 1.5 | 1 | B |
| Latent, after SIGReg | 0.75 | 1 | A |

The task wants B, but the planner picks A. The predictor is not to blame: it can know
exactly where each plan ends. The latent space measures distance with a different ruler
than the task.

Measuring every direction in units of its own spread is a sensible rule for some uses: it
says how unusual a miss is. It is not the rule of a task that counts metres. When the goal
can be reached exactly, the two rules agree, since both are zero at the goal. They disagree
on near misses, which is where the planner ends up when the goal is out of reach.

### From the vacuum to any world

The vacuum was chosen so that its spreads line up with the walls. In a real world model the
state has many coordinates, and the directions in which it varies a lot can be any mix of
them: a robot arm whose shoulder and elbow tend to move together, for example. To describe
the spread in every direction at once, we use the covariance $\Sigma$ of the training
states. For the vacuum it holds the two variances and nothing else:

$$
\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 2^2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1^2 \end{pmatrix}
$$

Here the diagonal entries are the variances left–right and up–down, and the zeros say the
two coordinates vary independently. In a general world the off-diagonal entries are not
zero, and they record which coordinates move together.

Now look at the vacuum's squared latent distance from a point $x$ to the goal $x_g$. Each
error is divided by its spread before squaring, so it is divided by its variance after
squaring:

$$
\Big(\frac{\Delta x_1}{2}\Big)^2 + \Big(\frac{\Delta x_2}{1}\Big)^2
= \frac{\Delta x_1^2}{4} + \frac{\Delta x_2^2}{1}
= \Delta x^\top \Sigma^{-1} \Delta x
$$

Here $\Delta x = x - x_g$ is the error vector, with values $\Delta x_1$ left–right and
$\Delta x_2$ up–down, and $\Sigma^{-1}$ is the inverse of $\Sigma$, which for the vacuum is just
$\mathrm{diag}(1/4, 1)$: one over each variance. In words: the latent space measures every
error in units of how much the state varies in that direction, so errors count less in
directions that varied more. This holds for any linear encoder whose latents are
isotropic:

{

A linear encoder $z = Ax$ turns an error $\Delta x = x - x_g$ into the latent cost

$$
\|A\,\Delta x\|^2 = \Delta x^\top M\, \Delta x, \qquad M = A^\top A
$$

Here $A$ is the encoder's matrix and $M$ the metric it induces on states. Let $A$ be
invertible and $\Sigma$ the state covariance (the real-world spread of the
training states in every direction). If the encoded covariance is isotropic
$\Leftrightarrow A \Sigma A^\top = sI$ for some $s > 0$, then $M = s\,\Sigma^{-1}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A \Sigma A^\top &= sI \\
\Sigma &= s\, A^{-1} (A^\top)^{-1} = s\, (A^\top A)^{-1} \\
\Sigma^{-1} &= \tfrac{1}{s}\, A^\top A \\
M = A^\top A &= s\, \Sigma^{-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

Putting $M$ back into the latent cost gives:

$$
\|A(x - x_g)\|^2 = (x - x_g)^\top M\, (x - x_g) = s\,(x - x_g)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (x - x_g)
$$

Consequently, the latent cost ranks plans like the task only if the task also weighs each
direction by one over its variance. The vacuum's task weighs every direction the same, so
the two rankings can differ, as they do for A and B.

}{cfyb card: Lemma 1 (explained) — isotropic latents weight errors by inverse covariance}

So far we assumed the latents are exactly isotropic. A trained world model is not built
that way: SIGReg only pushes the latents towards isotropy, while the prediction loss shapes
them too. So does training actually end up with isotropic latents? In the linear case,
Proposition 1 says yes: the trained model ends up with the same squeezed ruler as the
vacuum, $\Sigma^{-1}$:

{

Take the simplest world: everything is linear and Gaussian, and every step adds a little
random noise of size $\eta$. Train a linear encoder and a linear predictor with prediction
loss plus SIGReg, and look at the best possible result of training. It has:

- No collapse: the encoder keeps all information about the state, even though training is
  free to collapse.
- Perfect prediction: the predictor is as accurate as the noise allows, and as $\eta$
  shrinks, the prediction loss goes to zero.
- The squeezed ruler: as $\eta$ shrinks, the latent distance to the goal becomes
  $q\,(x - x_g)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (x - x_g)$ for some scale $q > 0$, the same ruler as for
  isotropic latents.

So the model is as good as it can be on both losses, and still measures distance like the
vacuum's latent space.

}{cfyb card: Proposition 1 (explained) — joint training picks the inverse-covariance metric}

So a well-trained model ends up with the vacuum's ruler. How much does this ruler cost the
planner? Let $J(U)$ be the task cost of a plan $U$: the real distance from where the plan
ends to the goal. The regret of a plan is how much worse it is than the best plan:

$$
\mathrm{Regret}(U) = J(U) - \min_{U'} J(U')
$$

Here $U$ is the plan the latent planner chose, and the minimum runs over all plans $U'$ it
could have chosen. The regret is zero when the planner picks the best plan. (The authors square the
distance, which changes the size of the regret but not which plan is best.) For the vacuum,
the planner picks A, 1.5 m from the goal, while B is 1 m away, so the regret is
$1.5 - 1 = 0.5$ m. Theorem 1 shows that this gap stays even when prediction becomes
perfect:

{

For any dimension $D \geq 2$, any finite horizon $H$ and any state covariance $\Sigma$
that is not a multiple of the identity, there is a linear control problem with bounded
actions in which a model trained with prediction loss plus SIGReg plans worse than it
should. As the noise $\eta$ shrinks, the model predicts better and better, one step ahead
and over the whole horizon, but the regret of its plans does not shrink to zero: it settles
at a positive value $\Delta_H$.

The reason: the end points the planner can reach form a ball. The task picks the point of
the ball closest to the goal in plain Euclidean distance, while the latent planner picks
the closest point under the squeezed ruler $\Sigma^{-1}$. For a goal outside the ball,
unless it lies exactly along one of the main directions of $\Sigma$, these two points
differ. The vacuum's choice between A and B is a small version of this.

}{cfyb card: Theorem 1 (explained) — perfect prediction, positive regret}

To check that the same happens with a neural network,
the authors train an MLP encoder and a predictor with prediction loss plus SIGReg on a
two-dimensional system, much like the vacuum: the actions and the noise are the same in
every direction, but the dynamics make the states spread more in some directions than in
others. The goals are placed out of reach, so the planner has to choose a near miss. They
repeat the training for several noise levels $\eta$, with ten seeds each:

Since the problem is the ruler and not the predictor, the fix has to change the ruler. To
do this, the authors propose ΛReg, which keeps SIGReg's Gaussian target but lets training
choose how much each direction is squeezed.

## ΛReg: a Gaussian with learned variances

AnisoWM replaces SIGReg's target $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$ with $\mathcal{N}(0, \Lambda)$, where
$\Lambda$ is a learned diagonal covariance with two constraints:

$$
\Lambda = \mathrm{diag}(v_1, \dots, v_D), \qquad \mathrm{tr}\,\Lambda = D, \qquad \frac{\max_i v_i}{\min_i v_i} \leq \kappa
$$

Here $D$ is the latent dimension, $v_i$ the target variance of coordinate $i$, and $\kappa$
the largest allowed ratio between two target variances. The trace keeps the total
variance the same as SIGReg's, and $\kappa = 1$ gives back the isotropic target.

The regulariser itself is a one-line change. Divide every coordinate by its target
standard deviation, and run plain SIGReg on the result:

$$
\Lambda\mathrm{Reg}(Z) = \mathrm{SIGReg}\big(\Lambda^{-1/2} Z\big)
$$

Here $Z$ is a batch of latents. If $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Lambda)$, then
$\Lambda^{-1/2} z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, so SIGReg's test against a standard normal can
be reused unchanged. The division happens only inside the regulariser: the predictor and
the planner see the original latents.

The training loss is LeWorldModel's, with SIGReg swapped for ΛReg:

$$
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}} + \lambda \cdot \Lambda\mathrm{Reg}(Z)
$$

Here $\mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}}$ is the next-latent prediction loss and $\lambda$ the weight
of the regulariser. Planning does not change at all: it is still the Euclidean cost $J_z$
from above. What changes is that ΛReg, unlike SIGReg, has trainable parameters: the $D$
target variances $v_1, \dots, v_D$. To better understand all the steps, you can look at the
code below.

{

The variances are stored as $D$ logits $\alpha_i$, with
$v_i = D\,\mathrm{softmax}(\alpha)_i$, so they always sum to $D$. The logits start at
zero, so training starts from SIGReg's isotropic target, and after every optimization step they are
clipped to $[-\tfrac{1}{2}\log\kappa, \tfrac{1}{2}\log\kappa]$, which keeps the ratio
between any two variances at most $\kappa$.

> *Note: the code is adapted from the paper's Algorithm 1 (Appendix F.1). The SIGReg part
> is replaced by a call, to make the ΛReg part easy to read, and the clipping is added,
> which the paper describes only in the text.*

```python
def LambdaReg(x, alpha, global_step, num_slices=1024):
    """x is (N, D); alpha is a learnable vector of size D."""

    # --- learned diagonal Gaussian target ---
    D = x.size(1)
    v = D * alpha.softmax(dim=0)   # sum(v) = D

    # standardize only inside LambdaReg
    x = x * v.rsqrt()

    # --- original SIGReg statistic ---
    return SIGReg(x, global_step, num_slices)


# after every optimizer step: keeps max(v) / min(v) <= kappa
with torch.no_grad():
    alpha.clamp_(-0.5 * math.log(kappa), 0.5 * math.log(kappa))
```

}{cfyb code: Code — ΛReg, adapted from Algorithm 1}

So during training, ΛReg gives us the freedom to pick a variance for each direction,
enabling the system itself to decide how to spread them. Moreover, it can still learn the
isotropic Gaussian if needed: we do not lose the isotropic target, we just give the system
more room to choose. To analyse which directions the system picks, and why, the authors
study the linear case in their Proposition 2:

{

Take the same linear Gaussian world as in Proposition 1, but train with ΛReg instead of
SIGReg, with a bound $\kappa > 1$. As the noise $\eta$ shrinks, the best possible result of
training still has no collapse and perfect prediction, as before. What changes is the
ruler. The latent distance to the goal becomes:

$$
q\,(x - x_g)^\top \Sigma^{-1/2} L\, \Sigma^{-1/2} (x - x_g)
$$

Here $L$ is the learned target covariance, seen in state coordinates rescaled to unit
spread; its variances still sum to $D$ and differ by at most a factor $\kappa$. With
$L = I$ this is SIGReg's ruler $\Sigma^{-1}$. Training picks the $L$ that minimises:

$$
\mathrm{tr}(LR), \qquad R = \Sigma^{-1/2}\, W\, \Sigma^{-1/2}
$$

Here $W$ is the covariance of the noise, so $R$ is the noise measured in units of the
state's own spread. Minimising $\mathrm{tr}(LR)$ puts more variance where the noise is
small compared to how much the state moves: the directions that are easiest to predict.

For a task that weighs every direction the same, the ideal is $L$ proportional to
$\Sigma$, which cancels $\Sigma^{-1}$ and gives back the task's ruler. In the simplest case,
where the noise is the same in every direction and $\Sigma$ has only two different
variances, training picks exactly this $L$ when $\kappa$ equals the ratio of the two
variances, and the regret goes to zero. A larger $\kappa$ overshoots: the regret grows
again, and with enough anisotropy it becomes worse than with SIGReg.

}{cfyb card: Proposition 2 (explained) — prediction chooses the allocation}

Back to the vacuum, whose wheels slip a little, by the same amount in every direction.
ΛReg has a fixed budget: two target variances $l_1$ (left–right) and $l_2$ (up–down) that
sum to 2 and differ by at most a factor $\kappa$. Every bit of variance given to a
direction also stretches the slip in it, which no predictor can foresee, so the prediction
loss pays for it. The slip is the same both ways, but the vacuum moves four times more left–right
(in variance), so in units of its spread the slip left–right is four times smaller. The price of the budget is:

$$
\mathrm{tr}(LR) \propto \frac{l_1}{4} + l_2
$$

Variance left–right is four times cheaper, so training gives it as much as the bound
allows. With $\kappa = 2$ this is $l_1 = 4/3$ and $l_2 = 2/3$, and a metre left–right
becomes $\sqrt{l_1}/2 \approx 0.58$ units, while a metre up–down becomes
$\sqrt{l_2} \approx 0.82$ units. The squeeze is now much milder than SIGReg's 0.5 versus 1.
With $\kappa = 4$, the ratio of the two variances, both become $\approx 0.63$ units: the
task's own ruler up to scale:

| Distance to the goal | A (1.5 m left) | B (1 m below) | Closer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Real, in metres | 1.5 | 1 | B |
| Latent, after SIGReg | 0.75 | 1 | A |
| Latent, after ΛReg with $\kappa = 2$ | 0.87 | 0.82 | B |
| Latent, after ΛReg with $\kappa = 4$ | 0.95 | 0.63 | B |

Training never saw the goal or the task cost. It only saw that the vacuum moves more
left–right than up–down, while slipping equally in both. That this leads to the task's
ruler rests on two facts of the example: the slip is the same in every direction, and the
task counts metres.

Putting it together, the bound $\kappa$ limits how unequal the variances can become, and the
prediction loss decides which directions get the larger ones. With $\kappa = 1$ we are back
to SIGReg. A larger $\kappa$ gives the system more freedom, but not always a better planner,
as the experiments below also show.

## Results

AnisoWM is tested on the four environments of LeWorldModel (TwoRoom, Reacher, PushT and
OGBench Cube), with its data, architecture (192-dimensional latents) and planning protocol,
and the same regulariser weight $\lambda = 0.09$. One bound, $\kappa = 2$, is used everywhere,
so no target variance can be more than twice another. The LeWorldModel baseline is
retrained in the same pipeline with the released code ($\kappa = 1 \Leftrightarrow \mathrm{SIGReg}$). Each model is trained
with three seeds and evaluated on 50 start and goal pairs per environment, with a budget of
50 steps.

### Does the latent cost order plans like the task does?

The theory says the latent ruler can put plans in the wrong order, like A before B for the
vacuum. So the authors test the order directly. For each start and goal, they take at least
eight recorded action sequences from earlier runs, each with the place it really ended, and
measure how far that is from the goal. Then they compare the sequences two at a time. Say one
sequence really ended 1 m from the goal and another 2 m away: the latent cost gets the pair
right if it also gives the first one the lower cost. The score is the fraction of pairs it
gets right.

Each sequence $U$ is scored with two latent costs:

$$
J_{\text{enc}}(U) = \big\| f(o_H(U)) - z_g \big\|^2, \qquad
J_{\text{pred}}(U) = \big\| \hat z_H(U) - z_g \big\|^2
$$

Here $f$ is the encoder, $o_H(U)$ the frame the sequence really ended in, $\hat z_H(U)$ the
predictor's guess of the final latent, and $z_g$ the latent of the goal. $J_{\text{enc}}$
involves no predictor, so it tests the latent space alone. $J_{\text{pred}}$ is the cost
the planner actually uses.

| Environment | $J_{\text{enc}}$ LeWM | $J_{\text{enc}}$ AnisoWM | $J_{\text{pred}}$ LeWM | $J_{\text{pred}}$ AnisoWM |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| TwoRoom | 0.564 | 0.661 | 0.575 | 0.646 |
| Reacher | 0.790 | 0.893 | 0.676 | 0.723 |
| PushT | 0.647 | 0.641 | 0.594 | 0.621 |
| Cube | 0.555 | 0.576 | 0.537 | 0.553 |

With the cost the planner uses, $J_{\text{pred}}$, AnisoWM orders plans better in all four
environments. With the latent space alone, $J_{\text{enc}}$, it is clearly better in
TwoRoom and Reacher (about +0.1), slightly better in Cube, and no better in PushT, where
the gain appears only once the predictor is involved.

{

I found this work quite impressive, and I think it opens a new front of research: the
design of the target, whether a distribution or a more general rule on the shape of the
latent space. I expect to see more alternatives to SIGReg. SIGReg has
theory behind it, yet the theorems that argue for the isotropic Gaussian either rely on
strong assumptions or are proven in the linear case, with a few rare nonlinear exceptions,
and in practice we hardly use such
models. The theorems and propositions here are also built on the linear
case, with only a toy MLP check beyond it.

What I would like to see instead are target distributions with more freedom to take a
shape, while still
preventing collapse. Something like a mixture of Gaussians or a mixture density network
could work, and it could still be checked with the same random projections, since every
one-dimensional projection of a mixture of Gaussians is again a mixture of Gaussians. A clever way of avoiding collapse may then be needed, though.

I would also like to see more empirical tests and careful analysis of these models, to
understand better how they work and where their limits are. Instead of theorems that hold
only under narrow assumptions, I would prefer either more empirical results or one strong
theorem.
